Predictor Budget

In a retrospective or routinely collected cohort the sample size is already fixed. The question becomes how many candidate predictors you can afford. Answer four questions and this tool works Riley's minimum sample size criteria backwards to give you that number.

Showing an example. Replace it with your own cohort.

1What kind of outcome?

For a continuous outcome use pmsampsize(type = "c") in R or Python directly. If everyone is followed to a fixed time with no censoring, a binary model is usually the right choice.

2Your cohort

Total follow-up is the sum of every patient's time to event or censoring. The horizon is the time your predictions are for, such as 2-year or 5-year survival. Neither changes the parameter budget; they only feed the precision check. Count only patients whose outcome is known. Events are the patients who had the outcome.
How do I get these numbers from my data? 

            Use the same outcome definition and censoring as the model you will fit. If you have competing risks, this calculation does not account for them.

If you're over budget

        
    
          
  1. Cut candidates on prior knowledge, before touching the outcome data. Use clinical reasoning and the literature to drop predictors that are unlikely to add much, are hard to measure, or overlap with others (Riley et al. 2020).
    2. 
          
  2. Spend fewer degrees of freedom per predictor. Use fewer spline knots or linear terms for weaker predictors, merge sparse categories, and leave out interactions that aren't pre-specified (Harrell 2015, ch. 4).
    3. 
          
  3. Reduce data without looking at the outcome. Combine correlated predictors into a summary score or principal component chosen blind to the outcome (Harrell 2015, ch. 4).
    4. 
          
  4. Don't count on penalisation to rescue a small sample. Lasso, ridge and similar methods shrink in the right direction on average, but the amount of shrinkage is itself unreliable when data are small (Riley et al. 2021; Van Calster et al. 2020).
    5. 
          
  5. Consider updating or validating an existing model instead of developing a new one, or pooling with other cohorts (Riley et al. 2020).

How this works

        
    
          
  • Criterion 1 keeps expected overfitting small: the model's uniform shrinkage factor should be at least 0.9. Criterion 2 keeps apparent and adjusted Nagelkerke R² within 0.05 of each other. Criterion 3 asks for a precise estimate of overall risk. For binary outcomes it sets a minimum number of patients. For survival it only checks the precision of the risk at your horizon and never raises the sample size.
    • 
          
  • A parameter is a degree of freedom. A binary or linear predictor uses 1. A categorical predictor with k levels uses k − 1. A restricted cubic spline with k knots uses k − 1. Fractional polynomials count each power estimated: FP1 is 2 and FP2 is 4. Every interaction adds its own.
    • 
          
  • Working backwards. For each R², the tool raises the number of parameters one at a time until the required sample size exceeds your cohort. The last count that fits is your budget.
    • 
          
  • R² from a previous model is shrunk for optimism with van Houwelingen's heuristic, S = 1 − df / LR, and R²CS,adj = S × R²CS,app (Riley et al. 2019, eqs. 3–8).
    • 
          
  • Accuracy. The criteria reproduce pmsampsize 1.1.3 exactly. They are tested against the R package on 685 input combinations, and on the worked examples in Riley et al. 2019. The binary C-statistic conversion is a deterministic version of pmsampsize's simulation and agrees with it to about ±0.001.
